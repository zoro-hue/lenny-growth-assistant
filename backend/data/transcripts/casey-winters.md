# Casey Winters on Growth Loops, Retention, and Why Blended Metrics Lie | Episode 42

## Transcript

**Lenny Rachitsky** (00:00:05):
Welcome to Lenny's Podcast. Today my guest is Casey Winters. Casey has worked with and advised some of the fastest-growing consumer and B2B products in the world, including Pinterest, Eventbrite, Grubhub, and Canva. Casey, welcome to the show.

**Casey Winters** (00:00:22):
Thanks Lenny, really excited to be here.

**Lenny Rachitsky** (00:00:26):
Let's start with product-market fit. You have a very specific, quantitative view of how companies deceive themselves into thinking they have PMF when they really just have temporary acquisition bursts. How should founders and growth operators measure true PMF?

**Casey Winters** (00:00:44):
Product-market fit is revealed when a cohort retention curve stops dropping and runs completely flat parallel to the horizontal axis. If you don't have that horizontal floor by Month 3 or Month 6, nothing else you do matters. You cannot optimize your way out of a leaky bucket. The biggest failure in growth teams is attempting to optimize top-of-funnel conversion before understanding where users naturally find ongoing habit value.

**Lenny Rachitsky** (00:01:15):
When you say a cohort retention curve must flatten, what percentage retention floor should people look for? Does it vary by product type?

**Casey Winters** (00:01:25):
It varies substantially by business model. In consumer subscription or social, a flat retention curve at 20% to 30% can build a massive business if your top-of-funnel is enormous and organic. In B2B SaaS, your logo retention floor needs to be 70% or 80%, and your Net Revenue Retention (NRR) should be over 110% to 120% because existing accounts expand over time.

**Lenny Rachitsky** (00:01:54):
You're well-known for pioneering the concept of growth loops versus traditional linear funnels. Can you break down why linear funnels are dead?

**Casey Winters** (00:02:04):
Linear funnels—where you put a dollar into Facebook or Google ads, get a user, and that user reaches the end of the funnel—suffer from diminishing returns. As you spend more, your cost per acquisition rises and user quality declines. A growth loop is a closed system: the output of one cycle becomes the input for the next cycle. For example, at Pinterest, a user searches on Google, lands on a Pin, signs up, saves five more Pins, and those five Pins get indexed by Google, attracting the next ten users. That is a content loop, and it compounds automatically without paying ad networks.

**Lenny Rachitsky** (00:02:49):
What are the primary archetypes of sustainable growth loops you see across modern tech companies?

**Casey Winters** (00:02:57):
There are three foundational archetypes:
First, viral and network loops. The user uses the product to communicate with or invite someone else—think Figma, Zoom, or Slack.
Second, user-generated content and SEO loops. Users create content that search engines index or that gets distributed across social channels—think Pinterest, Yelp, or Stack Overflow.
Third, paid reinvestment loops. You generate cash from customer transactions and reinvest that cash immediately into customer acquisition channels with positive unit economics. But paid loops only work if your payback period is under 12 months.

**Lenny Rachitsky** (00:03:42):
What is your advice for product teams that are seeing their CAC increase and retention drop?

**Casey Winters** (00:03:48):
Stop spending on acquisition immediately. Go talk to the 5% or 10% of users who are active every week. Find out what specific workflow they rely on, and rebuild your onboarding funnel to get new users to that specific 'Aha' moment within their first session. Growth is about habit formation, not vanity traffic.
